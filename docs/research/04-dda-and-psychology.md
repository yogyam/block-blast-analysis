# 04 — Dynamic difficulty, retention design, incentives, patents, and the psychology of "one more game"

Written 2026-11-09 from `scouting/02-prior-art-scout.md` §2 and §4, re-verified against primary sources: the Hamlet PDF was read in full; abstracts for paywalled papers were pulled through the Semantic Scholar API by DOI; Google Patents pages were read for the EA and Sony patents and its JSON endpoint queried for Hungry Studio and Aretis Limited; the Royal Match complaint and King's F-1 were read directly. Tags follow `README.md`: **[C]** primary or two independent sources, **[R]** one credible secondary source, **[S]** forum/SEO/inference.

The question this document answers is not "is Block Blast rigged" (that is `02-rigged-discourse.md`) but "why would a developer build an adaptive dealer, what has been written about doing so, and what does each strand of that writing predict about our hypotheses H1–H8".

## 1. Dynamic difficulty adjustment: the literature

**Hunicke, R. and Chapman, V. (2004). "AI for Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment in Games." AAAI Workshop on Challenges in Game AI, San José (Technical Report WS-04-04).** PDF: https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/pubs/Hamlet.pdf [C; venue [R]]. The field's canonical system. Hamlet, a library inside Valve's Half-Life engine, monitors game statistics, "estimates the player's future state", and intervenes "when an undesirable *but avoidable* state is predicted". The target state is "flailing", modelled with inventory theory: cumulative damage is Gaussian by the CLT, so the probability of a health shortfall is an error integral the engine evaluates live. Interventions are **reactive** (entities "on stage": enemy accuracy, damage, health) or **proactive** (entities "in the wings": spawn order, type, health packed on bodies); reactive changes "run the risk of disrupting the player's sense of disbelief", proactive ones "are less likely to interrupt the player's suspension of disbelief". Policies are "modes of control": the **Comfort Zone** holds a player near 50% health "much like a benevolent babysitter"; the **Discomfort Zone** holds an expert at 15–20% "like an aggressive drill sergeant". Intervention cost is offset by "how often they've died" and "how many times we've intervened in the past". The framing is Csikszentmihalyi's flow channel; the goal is to "intervene *just enough*". Every mechanism our hypotheses need is here: a state monitor (H1), a help policy (H2), a hinder policy (H3), and a preference for interventions the player cannot see.

**Hunicke, R. (2005). "The case for dynamic difficulty adjustment in games." Proc. ACM SIGCHI International Conference on Advances in Computer Entertainment Technology (ACE 2005), Valencia, pp. 429–433. doi:10.1145/1178477.1178573.** Bibliographic details and a 483-citation count are confirmed via Semantic Scholar and the ACM DOI [C]; the ACM text was not accessible to us, so its content (an evaluation of Hamlet with players) is cited here only as the standard follow-up [S].

**Zohaib, M. (2018). "Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment (DDA) in Computer Games: A Review." Advances in Human-Computer Interaction, vol. 2018, article 5681652, 12 pp. doi:10.1155/2018/5681652.** Abstract [C]: "Dynamic difficulty adjustment (DDA) is a method of automatically modifying a game's features, behaviors, and scenarios in real-time, depending on the player's skill, so that the player, when the game is very simple, does not feel bored or frustrated, when it is very difficult. The intent of the DDA is to keep the player engrossed till the end." It contrasts DDA with difficulty that "increase[s] linearly or stepwise" and is set "only at the beginning of the game". The review is organised by technique (probabilistic models, perceptrons, dynamic scripting, reinforcement learning, bandits, affective sensing) [R]. Its implicit assumption, shared by nearly everything it reviews, is that adjustment serves the player's experience; monetisation is not a variable.

**Paraschos, P. D. and Koulouriotis, D. E. (2023). "Game Difficulty Adaptation and Experience Personalization: A Literature Review." International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction 39(1):1–22 (online 2022). doi:10.1080/10447318.2021.2020008.** Abstract [C]: a Scopus-based systematic review of 109 papers, 2005–2021, "from the perspective of dynamic difficulty adjustment and procedural content generation", taxonomised by adaptation/personalisation approach; findings concern "the dynamic modeling of the players' characteristics" and benefits for "players/students and the rehabilitation of patients". Again: adaptation is framed as welfare-improving.

**Lopes, P., Fachada, N. and Fonseca, M. (2025; v2 2026). "Closing the Loop in Affect-Driven Game Adaptation: A Systematic Review." arXiv:2505.01351** (v1, 2 May 2025, was titled "Closing the Loop: A Systematic Review of Experience-Driven Game Adaptation") [C]. A PRISMA review of 23 empirical studies (2015–2025) that implement a complete loop: player data acquisition → player-experience model → adaptive content. Complete-loop systems "were relatively uncommon in the retrieved corpus"; game telemetry dominated as input; knowledge-based methods (rules, heuristics) dominated over machine learning "because of interpretability and low deployment requirements"; affect was mostly used to calibrate challenge. The academic loop is rule-based and telemetry-driven, the shape of the ~700 JS "traits" the teardown describes; and no reviewed study measures retention or revenue, the gap §3 fills.

**Mechanism vocabulary.** *Rubber-banding* is the racing-game term for pulling trailing players forward and leaders back; it is DDA with a symmetric policy. *Helping vs hindering*: Hamlet has both policies, but the published literature overwhelmingly studies helping, and the hindering case is justified by flow (bored experts). Nothing in §1 predicts a policy that hinders to produce revenue; that prediction comes from §3 and §4.

## 2. Measuring difficulty with agents

**Wang, C.-J., Guo, J.-T., Guei, H., Shih, C.-C., Wu, T.-R. and Wu, I-C. (2026). "Evaluating Game Difficulty in Tetris Block Puzzle." arXiv:2603.18994 (GPW 2025).** [C] An 8×8 block puzzle, evaluated with Stochastic Gumbel AlphaZero, "a budget-aware planning agent for stochastic environments". Difficulty is measured as training reward (higher = easier) and iterations to convergence (faster = easier). Holding a block and previewing the held block reduce difficulty; adding Tetris block variants increases it, with the T-pentomino producing "the largest slowdown". This is the closest academic treatment of our exact board; it also shows that piece-set composition alone moves difficulty, which is why our piece-set derivation must be pre-registered.

**Jiang, J. (2026). "OpenBlock: Constructive and Verified Content Generation for Adaptive Tile-Matching Games." arXiv:2609.22177 (27 Aug 2026).** [C] An adaptive platform with a dual-track generator: a deterministic rule-based track plus an optional learned track, both behind a verification gate that uses "exhaustive sequential-placement search" to confirm every delivered piece set is fully placeable. A self-play RL agent (234,000+ episodes, 35.6% win rate, median score 4,200) diagnoses late-game failure: at 70–75% board fill, 33–56% of long-bar pieces have no legal placement. Spawn-difficulty distributions are "statistically indistinguishable" between won and lost games, so the author attributes failure to board degeneration rather than piece difficulty. A 14-day gray rollout to 48,000 players lifted day-1 retention by 1.8 percentage points and session duration by 7% over the rule track alone. Three lessons: a verified-solvable generator is a board-aware dealer by construction; the won-vs-lost comparison is the nearest published analogue of our conditional test, and it reported a null; +1.8 pp D1 is the size of effect a studio ships for.

**Kristensen, J. T., Valdivia, A. and Burelli, P. (2020). "Estimating player completion rate in mobile puzzle games using reinforcement learning." IEEE Conference on Games (CoG) 2020. doi:10.1109/CoG47356.2020.9231581; arXiv:2306.14626.** [C] In Lily's Garden, an RL agent's move counts per level, best predicted by its top ~5% runs, track human completion rates: the agent is not human-level in absolute terms, but "the differences in terms of behaviour between levels are highly correlated to the differences in human behaviour". **Kristensen, J. T. and Burelli, P. (2024). "Difficulty Modelling in Mobile Puzzle Games: An Empirical Study on Different Methods to Combine Player Analytics and Simulated Data." International Journal of Computer Games Technology 2024, article 5592373. doi:10.1155/2024/5592373; arXiv:2401.17436.** [C] "Models trained on a combination of cohort statistics and simulated data produce the most accurate estimations of difficulty in all scenarios." Agents are accepted proxies for human difficulty, which licenses a fixed-policy agent as our instrument, and studios already run such simulators in production (Hungry Studio's "Block AI", per `02-rigged-discourse.md`).

## 3. Incentives and the business case

**Pape, L.-D., Helmers, C., Iaria, A., Wagner, S. and Runge, J. (2025). "Personalized Content, Engagement, and Monetization in a Mobile Puzzle Game." International Journal of Industrial Organization 98, article 103128 (online Dec 2024). doi:10.1016/j.ijindorg.2024.103128.** The abstract, as reproduced on the Santa Clara University economics page [C]: using "detailed player-level data from a mobile puzzle game and a novel structural model of player behavior", the authors "investigate the effects on revenue of personalizing game difficulty using observable player characteristics"; "personalized difficulty leads to an overall increase in player engagement and, consequently, revenue generation"; the game's average difficulty is already set to maximise revenue, yet "personalization can further increase revenue by 71%"; the largest *relative* gain comes from the smallest spenders and the largest *absolute* gain from further spending by the largest spenders. This is a peer-reviewed economics paper, with a named mobile-games co-author, estimating that per-player difficulty is worth roughly three-quarters more revenue. It is the single most important incentive statement in this file.

**AppMagic (Kalashnikova, L., 2026-03-18). "Hybridcasual Puzzles: Turning Failure into Revenue."** https://appmagic.rocks/blog/fail-offers [C]. "Fail offers" are purchases "triggered after losing a level". Using AppMagic's IAP revenue-distribution tool (US iPhone, all time): Color Block Jam's $4.99 fail offer is "more than 20% of the game's total IAP revenue"; Pixel Flow's $5.99 fail offer is "around 33% of total IAP revenue" (an estimated ~$14M); Screwdom 14.5%; several Rollic titles over 25%. The loop in the article's words: "purchasing a fail offer feels far more appealing than losing hours of progress." Failure is the monetisation event in this genre; a dealer that can schedule failure can schedule revenue.

**Deconstructor of Fun (Katkoff, M., 2025-02-03). "Hybridcasual Puzzles: Expanding the Puzzle Market."** [C] Successful casual puzzles run "a 'roller coaster' of difficulty": "levels gradually increase in challenge until a 'hard' or 'super hard' level appears", then ease; this pattern has "proven key in keeping players engaged and driving conversions through end game offers". Katkoff recommends adding dynamic difficulty so that repeat-failers can progress "without causing frustration or churn", and says it "hasn't been implemented" in hybridcasual "as far as I can see". Block Blast is not mentioned. Note the tension with the teardown: a leading industry analyst believed in early 2025 that the category had not yet shipped DDA.

**Wooga at GDC 2014 (Steinhoff, F., "FUUU factor", Jelly Splash; PocketGamer.biz, 2014-03-17).** [C] A spiky curve: "Blocking Levels" with lose rates "up to 95 percent", "Relief Levels" to rebuild confidence, and a first blocking level in the first session because "Players need to lose, and lose early on". FUUU factor = tries ÷ near-wins (100 tries with 5 near-wins = 20); levels above 10 were discarded. Target luck share ~70%. Rules: "Make it difficult, keep it casual, and use more luck." A published recipe for manufacturing near-misses at scale, a decade before the teardown.

**Applixir (2026). "The Block Blast Playbook: What $127M in Ad Revenue Actually Bought."** [C, vendor marketing] Citing Sensor Tower: Block Blast! earned an estimated $127M in ad revenue in January–May 2026, the most of any mobile game. Placements: a persistent bottom banner, skippable interstitials between rounds, and **one rewarded-video placement "offered at the moment of failure in exchange for a revive", capped at one per session**; the article calls this "one of the least aggressive ad loads in its category" and makes no claim about difficulty. The structural point survives the marketing: the only opt-in, high-eCPM ad fires at game over, so every game-over has a price, and the price varies with the ad currently loaded. The scout's "Marbit" piece could not be located [S]. Vendor case studies (AdMob +8% retention, Moloco +10% Android ARPDAU, Digital Turbine +8% ARPDAU, PubMatic +8% banner ARPDAU) show how finely the lever is tracked [R].

## 4. Patents and the legal record

**EA, US 9,919,217 B2, "Dynamic difficulty adjustment."** Assignee Electronic Arts Inc.; inventors Aghdaie, Kolen, Mattar, Sardari, Xue, Zaman, Moss; filed 2016-03-08, granted 2018-03-20; pre-grant publication US 2017/0259177 A1. Read on Google Patents [C]. Claim 1 is a computer-implemented method that identifies a user, gathers interaction data, selects a parameter function (with a penalty on its complexity), predicts the user's churn rate, and "based at least in part on the predicted churn rate" performs "selecting a seed value for a knob associated with the video game" and adjusts the knob. The description defines the term: "the seed value is a value that initializes or influences a random or pseudorandom number generator." Dependent claims: claim 9 selects the seed from "a reason for the predicted churn rate"; claim 10, "modifying the execution of the video game comprises providing the seed value to the user computing device"; claims 16–17 identify a difficulty level from a retention probability and modify difficulty "by selecting a seed value from the one or more seed values". On visibility: "the modified execution of the video game is undetectable by the user", and the disclosed embodiments "perform dynamic difficulty adjustment using processes that are not detectable or are more difficult to detect by users". On triggers: "a churn rate above a particular threshold may cause a new seed value to be selected." A granted patent whose claims cover per-user churn prediction driving the RNG seed, with undetectability as a stated design goal. Its legal afterlife: *Zajonc v. Electronic Arts* (filed Nov 2020) alleged DDA in FIFA/Madden/NHL Ultimate Team; plaintiffs withdrew in March 2021 after technical access, and EA said the technology "never was in FIFA, Madden or NHL, and never will be" [C, multiple outlets and EA's own post].

**Chen, Z., Xue, S., Kolen, J., Aghdaie, N., Zaman, K. A., Sun, Y. and Seif El-Nasr, M. (2017). "EOMM: An Engagement Optimized Matchmaking Framework." WWW 2017; arXiv:1702.06820.** [C] Argues that "create fair games at all times" is not optimal for engagement, proposes matchmaking that maximises predicted engagement, shows equal-skill matching is a special case "under a highly simplified assumption that rarely holds in practice", and validates on data from an EA game. The same authors appear on the patent; the common thread is treating the player's next-session probability as the objective and game parameters as the controls.

**Sony, US 12,151,167 B2, "Adaptive difficulty calibration for skills-based activities in virtual environments."** Sony Interactive Entertainment; inventors Dorn, Bean, Juenger, Ramirez, Karimi, Azmandian; filed 2022-05-31, published 2023-11-30 as US 2023/0381662 A1, granted 2024-11-26 [C]. Compares a per-user, per-skill performance level to activity expectations and adjusts parameters (movement speed, delay, character strength, number of competitors) incrementally; data from one game can tune another. A conventional, player-serving DDA patent, showing the practice is mainstream enough to patent at platform level.

**Hungry Studio / Aretis Limited.** Google Patents' query endpoint returns zero results for assignee "Aretis Limited", assignee "Hungry Studio", and "Block Blast" with Aretis; searches restricted to patents.google.com, patents.justia.com, patentscope.wipo.int and uspto.gov return only a USPTO TTAB trademark proceeding (Aretis Limited, mark "Cubes Design", serial 98914653, filed 2025-08-13) [C, absence after search]. No patent describes their generator; nothing in the public record constrains it.

**Statements under legal exposure.** King's F-1 (filed 2014-02-18), under "Pricing transparency and consistency": "We keep the rules of the game economy identical throughout the game: the progression funnel is not narrowed through artificial blockers to force excessive social outreach or pay for progress" [C, EDGAR]. The Royal Match complaint (*Schudde v. Dream Games*, W.D. Wash. 2:24-cv-01215, filed 2024-08-08) alleges "on information and belief" that odds make "certain levels nearly impossible to pass initially" and are altered after repeated failures to "make levels easier to pass"; it cites no data, expert or code, and rests on Washington gambling law after *Kater v. Churchill Downs* [C]. The strongest denial on record is a securities filing; the strongest accusation is a pleading with no evidence attached. Neither side has published a conditional test.

## 5. Psychology: why "one more game" works

**Near-miss.** Reid, R. L. (1986), "The psychology of the near miss," Journal of Gambling Behavior 2:32–39, doi:10.1007/BF01019932, is the origin citation (264 citations on Semantic Scholar) [C bibliographic; text not accessed, so not summarised]. Clark, L., Lawrence, A. J., Astley-Jones, F. and Gray, N. (2009), "Gambling near-misses enhance motivation to gamble and recruit win-related brain circuitry," Neuron 61(3):481–490, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2008.12.031 [C]: 40 participants behaviourally, 15 in fMRI; near-misses were rated less pleasant than full misses yet increased the desire to continue, but only when the participant chose the gamble; they recruited ventral striatum and anterior insula, regions that also respond to wins; "near-misses invigorate gambling through the anomalous recruitment of reward circuitry". Larche, C. J., Musielak, N. and Dixon, M. J. (2017), "The Candy Crush Sweet Tooth: How 'Near-misses' in Candy Crush Increase Frustration, and the Urge to Continue Gameplay," Journal of Gambling Studies 33(2):599–615 (online 2016), doi:10.1007/s10899-016-9633-7 [C]: 60 avid players, 30 minutes, heart rate and skin conductance; near-misses (missing the level by one or two moves) "were more arousing than losses", "subjectively rated as the most frustrating of all outcomes", and "triggered the most substantial urge to continue play"; they "may contribute to players playing longer than intended". For Block Blast the near-miss is a game-over a few hundred points short of the personal best, with a revive offer on screen.

**Variable-ratio reinforcement and loot boxes.** Zendle, D. and Cairns, P. (2018), "Video game loot boxes are linked to problem gambling: Results of a large-scale survey," PLOS ONE 13(11):e0206767 [C]: n = 7,422; loot-box spending relates to problem-gambling severity with η² = 0.054 versus 0.004 for other microtransactions; causal direction unresolved. Drummond, A. and Sauer, J. D. (2018), "Video game loot boxes are psychologically akin to gambling," Nature Human Behaviour 2:530–532, doi:10.1038/s41562-018-0360-1 [R]: 45% of 22 games met all five gambling criteria; the named mechanism is variable-ratio reinforcement. A piece dealer is a variable-ratio schedule by nature; an adaptive one can tune the ratio.

**Loss-chasing.** Zhang, K. and Clark, L. (2020), "Loss-chasing in gambling behaviour: neurocognitive and behavioural economic perspectives," Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences 31:1–7, doi:10.1016/j.cobeha.2019.10.006 [C]: loss-chasing is "the tendency of a gambler to amplify their betting in an effort to recoup prior losses", "a hallmark of the transition from recreational to disordered gambling", with a special significance of "breaking even". Banerjee, N., Chen, Z., Clark, L. and Noël, X. (2023), "Behavioural expressions of loss-chasing in gambling: A systematic scoping review," Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews 152:105377 [C]: studies define chasing between sessions (n = 39) or within sessions (n = 38, continuing or intensifying). "Breaking even" maps onto the high-score line; within-session chasing is the revive.

**The compulsion loop.** Hopson's 2001 Gamasutra "Behavioral Game Design" brought operant schedules into game design; Kim's "The Compulsion Loop Explained" (Game Developer) formalised anticipation → activity → reward [R]. Hamlet states the designer's version: keep the player "in engaging interaction loops, for the most appropriate period of time" [C].

**Metric vocabulary a reader needs** [R, Unity and GameAnalytics glossaries]. *D1/D7/D30 retention*: share of installers who return on day 1, 7, 30. *DAU*: daily active users. *ARPDAU*: daily revenue ÷ DAU. *eCPM*: ad revenue per 1,000 impressions; rewarded video is the highest-eCPM format and in Block Blast is offered only at failure. *LTV*: lifetime revenue per player. *Churn*: stopping play, the EA patent's objective. *Fail offer / revive*: the purchase or ad shown at game over. *Arm*: one variant of a remote A/B configuration. OpenBlock (+1.8 pp D1) and Pape et al. (+71% revenue) set the scale.

## Implications for our hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Mechanisms in the literature that predict it | Direction and notes |
|---|---|---|
| **H1 board-aware dealing** | Hamlet's state monitor and intervention loop [C]; OpenBlock's verification gate (a verified-solvable generator is board-aware by construction) [C]; the dffge552 and BlastIt helping generators (`03-prior-art.md`) [C]; EA claim 1 adjusts a "knob" including the RNG seed [C]; teardown traits `PlaceHabitAdaptPathTrait`, `AlgoFillSortEdgeTrait` [C, bundle notes]. | Predicted by every strand, but the literature predicts *helping* dependence; only §3–§4 predict hindering. The conditional-logit primary test is signed. OpenBlock's null is the result we must be powered to distinguish. |
| **H2 rescue / easing** | Hamlet Comfort Zone [C]; deceptive.design and Royal Match ¶61 allege easing after repeated failure [C, as allegations]; Katkoff recommends it [C]; Toon Blast impression [S]; teardown `FailStreakStrategyTrait`, `MaturityDevelopmentV2Trait`, `DifferentStrategyDieTrait` (a "later small-piece rescue"), `ClassNewDeathOptimizeTrait` [C bundle / R scout]. | Strongly predicted; the socially acceptable DDA. Prediction: cumulative hazard Λ > 1 below the personal best or after losses, especially for new or low-"maturity" players (interacts with H6). |
| **H3 kill near personal best** | Near-miss: a loss just short of the goal maximises urge to continue (Larche; Clark) [C]; Wooga's engineered near-wins and 95%-lose blocking levels [C]; loss-chasing's "breaking even" [C]; teardown `PersonalMarginBreakTrait`, `FastHandPunishTrait` [R, scout]; Hamlet's Discomfort Zone is the benign version [C]. | Predicted by psychology and the teardown, not by academic DDA. Prediction: hazard rises just beyond the stored high score, with the kill framed as a near-miss. Model score, streak and board features jointly (methodology note 3). |
| **H4 lethal unsolvable hands** | Teardown `ZHI_SI_TI` generator: two placeable pieces plus one no ordering can place [C, code read]; fail offers worth 14–33% of IAP revenue [C]; the revive ad as the one rewarded placement [C]. | Predicted only by economics and the teardown; Katkoff believed the category had no DDA. Prediction: jointly unplaceable terminal hands occur above their exact H0 rate q(B) (Λ < 1). Lowest power; the terminal hand must be captured. |
| **H5 within-hand dependence** | Tetris randomizers are deliberately non-i.i.d. and their entropy is measurable [C]; OpenBlock's gate rejects whole sets [C]; EmirUsta's clone samples without replacement [C]; teardown `ContinueSameMoreRoundLimitTrait` replaces repeated pieces [C, bundle notes]. | Predicted by engineering practice regardless of intent. Prediction: duplicate rate, size-sum variance and lag-k correlations differ from a with-replacement null; exact permutation tests apply. A positive H5 is not evidence of manipulation. |
| **H6 session / lifecycle adaptivity** | EA claim 1: churn prediction → seed [C]; EOMM [C]; Hamlet's costs keyed on deaths and prior interventions [C]; Pape et al.: personalisation on "observable player characteristics" worth +71% [C]; Kristensen's cohort statistics [C]; teardown newbie/maturity traits and tree models [R, scout]. | Predicted by the strongest commercial sources. Prediction: Z and Λ drift with games played, days since install and revive history; unconditional score trends are confounded by learning, so hold the policy fixed. |
| **H7 remote A/B arms** | Hungry Studio's >50 tests/week, >10,000 in 2025 [R, scout]; OpenBlock's gray rollout [C]; EA claims 10 and 16–17 send seeds and difficulty levels to the device [C]; bundle provenance shows arm 332243014 selected on the test install [C]. | A nuisance variable that can mask or mimic every other hypothesis: stratify by version and config snapshot, interleave installs over calendar time, expect heterogeneous effects. |
| **H8 ad-value-driven timing** | Teardown `RewardHighValueAdHarvestTrait` (three completed rewarded ads, loaded eCPM ≥ 3.5× last, score ≥ record + 1000) [C, script read]; Applixir: the revive ad fires at failure [C]; AppMagic: failure is the revenue event [C]; EA's patent treats monetisation as a knob objective [C]. | Predicted by no academic source; the teardown's novel claim. Needs the ad-load/eCPM event stream (Frida hooks, `05-tech-and-re-plan.md`); otherwise testable only as an H3/H4 residual among players who watch rewarded ads. |

Two framing conclusions. The literature divides cleanly: academic DDA predicts H1, H2, H5 and H6 in the *helping* direction and calls them good design; the patents, the economics and the teardown predict H3, H4 and H8 in the *hindering* direction, and our signed tests can tell these apart. And every prior public RNG analysis (`03-prior-art.md` §3) tested marginals, while the one state-conditional comparison on record (OpenBlock) found nothing: a conditional effect would be the first; a null would show what the teardown has not, how often the lethal path is actually taken.

## Confidence and gaps

- **Confirmed [C]:** Hamlet (PDF read); Zohaib, Paraschos & Koulouriotis, Zhang & Clark, Banerjee, Larche abstracts (Semantic Scholar by DOI); Lopes, Wang, OpenBlock, Kristensen 2020/2024 (arXiv); Pape et al. abstract incl. the 71% sentence (SCU page); AppMagic, Deconstructor of Fun, Wooga, Applixir (pages read); EA and Sony patents (Google Patents); EOMM; Zendle & Cairns; Clark et al.; FIFA suit withdrawal; King F-1 (EDGAR); Royal Match complaint (PDF read); no Hungry Studio/Aretis patents (three assignee queries, zero results).
- **Reported [R]:** Hunicke 2004 venue; Zohaib's technique taxonomy; Drummond & Sauer; Hopson/Kim compulsion-loop pieces; vendor case-study lifts; glossary definitions; teardown trait names that I did not read in the bundle (PersonalMarginBreak, FastHandPunish, FailStreakStrategy, MaturityDevelopmentV2) come from the scout.
- **Downgraded [S]:** Hunicke 2005 content (text inaccessible); Reid 1986 content (text inaccessible, secondary accounts conflict); "Marbit" piece (not found); Toon Blast easing.
- **New since the scout:** the EA patent's claim structure (seed in claim 1, device delivery in claim 10, difficulty-level seeds in 16–17); OpenBlock's 48,000-player rollout and won/lost null; AppMagic per-game percentages; Wooga's FUUU formula; Katkoff seeing no DDA in the category in Feb 2025; Clark et al.'s personal-control moderator; the King F-1 context (pricing, not RNG).
- **Gaps:** no empirical paper measures a *hindering* DDA deployed for revenue; Pape et al.'s identification strategy not checked beyond the abstract; Royal Match docket status unverified on PACER; iOS evidence bundles unopened; no source quantifies rewarded-video eCPM for Block Blast specifically.
